Greece is almost out of lignite, and now exports electricity

Lignite made 54% of Greek electricity in 2010 and 4.7% in 2025, according to Ember. The largest lignite plant, Agios Dimitrios, closed on 15 May 2026, and the last unit is due to stop burning lignite by end-2026. Wind and solar made 40.6% of generation in 2025, against 30.2% in the EU, but gas rose to 40.3% and the renewables share slipped for a second year.

Lignite went from more than half of Greek power to under 5% in 15 years

Lignite, a soft brown coal mined in western Macedonia, generated 4.7% of Greek electricity in 2025, down from 54% in 2010, according to the energy think tank Ember.1

In absolute terms, lignite plants produced 2.7 TWh in 2025, against 30.8 TWh in 2010. For decades lignite was the backbone of the Greek grid, because it was the one fuel Greece had in quantity at home.

The last plants

Agios Dimitrios near Kozani, the largest lignite plant in the country at almost 1.6 GW across five units, shut down on 15 May 2026, after 42 years in operation.2 That left one lignite unit running: Ptolemaida 5, a 660 MW plant that is also the newest.

PPC, the majority-private, state-linked power company that owns the plants, says Ptolemaida 5 will stop burning lignite by the end of 2026 and be converted to gas.3 The grid operator IPTO asked PPC to keep the unit available in reserve until March 2027, and PPC's chief executive Georgios Stassis said this meant postponing decommissioning for a few months, not keeping it running.2

What this number doesn't say

Lignite has not gone to zero yet: Ptolemaida 5 still produced power in 2026, and IPTO data show 2,058 GWh of lignite generation from January to September 2026.7

Sun and wind replaced lignite, and gas filled the gaps

In 2025 wind and solar made 40.6% of Greek electricity, gas made 40.3% and hydro 5.9%, according to Ember.1

The chart shows the swap. Lignite's fall after 2019 was matched first by gas, which jumped to 40.5% in 2020, and then by solar and wind, which overtook gas in 2023. Hydro moves with rainfall, so it swings from year to year and was low in 2024 and 2025.

Solar capacity has more than tripled since 2020

Installed solar capacity went from 3.2 GW in 2020 to 11.1 GW at the end of 2025, and wind from 4.1 GW to 5.7 GW, by Ember's count.1 The grid operator IPTO said in October 2026 that renewable capacity, excluding large hydro, had passed 19 GW and should reach almost 20 GW by the end of 2026.8

Ahead of the EU on wind and solar

Greece got 40.6% of its electricity from wind and solar in 2025, against 30.2% for the EU as a whole.1 The two were level in 2010, at 5.1% and 5.5%, so the gap opened over the past decade.

In plain terms

Each kilowatt-hour of Greek electricity came with 325 grams of CO2 in 2025, less than half the 707 grams of 2010. That is still above the EU average of 211 grams, because gas and lignite remain in the mix and Greece has no nuclear power.1

Why the renewables share slipped two years running

On Ember's generation basis, renewables fell from 49.8% of Greek electricity in 2023 to 49.2% in 2024 and 47.7% in 2025, while gas rose from 32.7% to 40.3%.1

Three things drove it. Greece started exporting power, and the regulator linked the extra exports to higher gas-fired output.9 Hydro output was low, at 6.3% and 5.9%. And wind and solar output stopped growing even as capacity kept rising: solar generation was 11.4 TWh in 2025, almost the same as in 2024, although installed solar capacity rose by about a quarter.1

Curtailment: clean power thrown away

Curtailment is when the grid operator orders wind and solar farms to cut output, because the grid can't absorb or move the power at that moment. It happens most on sunny spring middays, when demand is low.

Curtailment doubled in 2025 to 1,867 GWh, or 6.6% of renewable output, and by September 2026 it had already passed the full-year 2025 figure.10,7 A team at Aristotle University of Thessaloniki forecast 3.3–3.7 TWh for the whole of 2026, about 12% of output.11 Hours with wholesale prices at or near zero also rose, from 186 in 2024 to 1,055.5 in the first nine months of 2026, which squeezes the income of solar farms that sell at market prices.

Batteries are only now arriving

Storage is the main fix, and it is late. Three subsidised battery auctions run by the energy regulator RAAEY awarded about 900 MW between 2023 and 2025.12,13 By the end of September 2026, IPTO counted 689 MW of batteries connected to the main grid, most of it in Thessaly.14 In October Metlen and Karatzis switched on a 330 MW battery in Larissa, the largest in Greece.15 Almost all of it was still in test operation, and the first units are expected to enter commercial operation in December 2026.16

Two measures, two answers

Eurostat, which tracks the EU's renewables target, puts Greece's renewable share of electricity at 51.2% in 2024 and a provisional 60.9% in 2025.17 That figure measures consumption, not generation, and it smooths out good and bad years for hydro and wind, so it does not show the slip. Both are valid, but they answer different questions, so this page does not chart them together. Ember's figures above describe what Greek power plants actually produced.

Greece now sells more electricity abroad than it buys

Greece was a net exporter of electricity in 2024 and 2025, after at least 14 years of net imports, and net exports reached 3.0 TWh in 2025, according to Ember and IPTO.1,18

Until 2023 Greece bought power from its neighbours every year, up to 9.9 TWh in 2019. In 2025 it exported 6.4 TWh and imported 3.4 TWh, and it was a net exporter in 11 of 12 months.18 The pace then picked up: IPTO's bulletin shows net exports of 6.7 TWh from January to September 2026, already more than twice the 2025 total.14

The power goes mainly north and west. In the first nine months of 2026 Bulgaria was the largest net buyer, followed by North Macedonia and Italy, according to IPTO's commercial schedules.14 The regulator RAAEY linked the 2025 export surge to an 8% rise in gas-fired generation.9

What this number doesn't say

Exports are good for the trade balance and for PPC and other generators, but part of what Greece sells is gas-fired power, which is one reason the domestic renewables share fell. Different bodies also count it differently: RAAEY put 2025 net exports at 2.7 TWh on a commercial basis, against IPTO's 3.0 TWh of physical flows.9,18

Cables to the islands, and bigger plans across the sea

Greece is wiring its islands to the mainland grid, and Crete has been fully connected since the 1,000 MW Crete–Attica cable entered commercial operation in December 2025.19

Many Greek islands still run, or ran until recently, on local oil-fired power stations, which are expensive and polluting. The cost is spread across all Greek consumers through a public-service charge on bills, so every island connection lowers a cost that the mainland pays too. IPTO credits the Crete–Attica link with stopping Crete's oil-fired units, saving about 1.5 million tonnes of CO2 a year.20 The same cables help island tourism, whose summer peaks set the islands' demand.

Greece has become a gas gateway for the Balkans and Ukraine

Greece now has two LNG terminals, Revithoussa near Athens and a 5.5 bcm-a-year floating terminal off Alexandroupolis, and it re-exports a growing share of the gas they bring in to its northern neighbours.39

LNG is liquefied natural gas, shipped by tanker and turned back into gas at a terminal. Gas generated 40.3% of Greek electricity in 2025, so it matters for power as much as for heating and industry.1 In 2025, 86.1% of the LNG landed at Revithoussa came from the United States.40

  • Revithoussa (operator DESFA): 225,000 m³ of storage, with its 2025 slots fully booked.41
  • Alexandroupolis FSRU (operator Gastrade): a floating storage and regasification unit that started commercial operation in October 2024. It went offline in January 2025 after a technical problem, resumed in August 2025, and was shut again for maintenance from April to June 2026.39,42,43
  • The IGB pipeline to Bulgaria, in operation since October 2022, carries 3 bcm a year and can be expanded to 5 bcm.44

The Vertical Corridor

The Vertical Corridor is a set of joint pipeline capacity products that let gas landed in Greece travel north through Bulgaria and Romania to Moldova and Ukraine. Early 2026 auctions drew little interest, so operators cut tariffs, and in July 2026 about 46% of annual capacity at the Greek–Bulgarian border point was booked for up to five years.45,46 North Macedonia and Serbia joined the initiative in September 2026.47

Greek gas exports nearly tripled in the first half of 2026, to 8.72 TWh from 2.86 TWh a year earlier, according to DESFA data reported by To Vima.48 The Greek state gas company DEPA signed its first deal to supply US LNG to Ukraine's Naftogaz in November 2025, and Atlantic-See, a joint venture of Aktor and DEPA, has a 20-year contract with the US exporter Venture Global for 1 million tonnes a year from 2030.49,50

What this number doesn't say

The gateway role depends on politics and prices. The EU's ban on Russian gas, which applies in stages from March 2026 to late 2027, creates demand for other supply in the region, but several 2026 auctions failed when gas was expensive.51,45 We could not find a confirmed figure for how much gas actually reached Ukraine through Greece.

Who is paying for it: Masdar, PPC and the grid operator

Two deals show where the money comes from: Masdar's purchase of Terna Energy at a €3.2bn enterprise value, completed in 2025, and PPC's sale of €4.25bn of new shares in May 2026.52,53,54

Masdar and Terna Energy

Masdar, the Abu Dhabi state-backed renewables company, agreed in June 2024 to buy Terna Energy at €20 a share, a €3.2bn enterprise value (the price of the equity plus its debt).52 It took control of 70% in November 2024 and reached 100% through a tender offer and squeeze-out in April 2025, when Terna Energy had 1,224 MW in operation.53 It targets 6 GW by 2030.55

Its projects include the Amfilochia pumped-storage plant in western Greece (680 MW), which the EIB valued at about €685m.56,57 In October 2026 Masdar agreed to buy the Enipeas solar plant in Thessaly, of more than 550 MWp, from Lightsource bp, with completion expected by the end of 2026.58 The deal is part of a wider wave of foreign direct investment into Greece.

PPC: from lignite utility to listed renewables group

PPC was a state-controlled, lignite-heavy utility until 2021. A €1.35bn share sale at €9 a share in 2021 cut the state's stake from 51% to about 34%, and a second raise in May 2026, at €18.63 a share, brought in €4.25bn of new shares and €250m from treasury shares, with the Qatar Investment Authority as an anchor investor.59,60,54,61 The state holds 33.4% after it.62 Both raises were sold on the Athens exchange, where PPC is listed.

The grid operator

IPTO's draft ten-year plan for 2027–2036 foresees about €9bn of grid investment, about 80% of it by 2030, for island links, cross-border cables and connecting more than 10 GW of new renewables.66

Is Greek electricity expensive?

Greek wholesale power has cost more than the EU average every year since 2015, but the gap is narrowing, and Greek households pay less per kilowatt-hour than the EU average in euros, though not once incomes are taken into account.67,68

In 2025 the Greek day-ahead price averaged €103.9/MWh, against €86.7/MWh for the 22 EU countries in Ember's data, which made Greece the sixth most expensive of the 22. Between 2015 and 2023 it was first or second every year.67 Gas plants set the Greek price whenever renewables can't cover demand, so Greek prices follow gas.69

Prices rose again in autumn 2026 across Europe, with gas. The Greek monthly average reached €197.2/MWh in October 2026, against €169.5/MWh in Germany, and one October day-ahead auction cleared at €247.82/MWh, the highest since 2023.67,70 The European Commission names the 2026 energy-price shock as one reason it expects Greek growth to slow.71

Purchasing power standards adjust for the fact that Greek incomes and prices are lower than the EU average. On that basis a Greek household's electricity costs slightly more than the EU average, and businesses pay slightly more even in plain euros.68,72

What this number doesn't say

Wholesale prices are only part of the bill, which also includes network charges, levies and taxes, and governments subsidised bills heavily during the 2022 crisis. The EU average here is a simple mean of countries, not weighted by consumption.

The takeaway

Greece has done the hard, visible part of the transition, closing lignite and building solar fast, and has turned from a power importer into an exporter. The next stage is less visible and slower: storage, grid and cables, which decide whether all that solar can be used rather than switched off.
