Greek credit is investment grade again, and Greece borrows almost like Germany

Greece is rated BBB, investment grade, after more than a decade as junk. Scope and DBRS restored investment grade in 2023, S&P and Fitch followed that year and Moody's in March 2025. Markets moved first: the 10-year spread over Germany averaged 0.78 points in 2025, against 21 points in 2012 (Eurostat), and bad loans fell to 3.4% of bank loans in June 2026, from 46% in 2017.

Greece is investment grade again, at every major agency

All five rating agencies that the European Central Bank uses now rate Greece investment grade: BBB at S&P, Fitch and DBRS, BBB+ at Scope and Baa3 at Moody's, the equivalent of BBB−.3 In 2015 and 2020 Greece was still rated junk.

A credit rating is an agency's opinion of how likely a borrower is to pay its debts in full and on time. The scales run from AAA down to D (default). Everything from AAA to BBB− (Baa3 at Moody's) is "investment grade"; BB+ and below is "speculative grade", which markets call junk.

Why the line between BBB− and BB+ matters

Investment grade changes who is allowed to lend to Greece, and on what terms. Three rules do most of the work.

  • Central-bank collateral. Banks can pledge government bonds to the ECB for cheap funding only if at least one accepted agency rates them at "credit quality step 3", roughly BBB− or better. The accepted agencies are DBRS, Fitch, Moody's, S&P and Scope.3 For years Greek bonds were accepted only through special waivers. The ECB withdrew one in February 2015, restored it in June 2016 and granted another in April 2020, during the pandemic.242548 Greek bonds now clear the bar on their own ratings.
  • Bond indices. Bloomberg's Global Aggregate index, a benchmark for global investment-grade funds, takes the middle rating of Moody's, S&P and Fitch and requires Baa3/BBB− or higher.4 On that rule, Greek bonds qualified once Fitch's upgrade on 1 Dec 2023 put two of the three at investment grade.
  • Fund mandates. Many pension funds, insurers and bond funds may hold only investment-grade debt, so a junk rating shuts out most of the money that buys government bonds. Investment grade lets that money back in.

Before the upgrades, the ECB had to make an exception even to buy Greek bonds. When it launched its pandemic purchase programme in March 2020, it waived the credit-quality rule for Greek government debt, which on its own would have kept Greek bonds out.26 The investment-grade rating ended Greece's dependence on such exceptions, and it is part of the same story as the stock market's move to developed-market status in 2026.

In plain terms

BBB is two notches above junk and two below the A range. Greece is not a top-rated borrower, but it is back in the group that mainstream investors can buy without special permission.

From A to default and back: the rating history since 2009

Greece fell from the A range to default between late 2009 and March 2012, stayed junk for 13 to 15 years at the big three agencies and climbed back between August 2023 and March 2025.

The downgrades tracked the debt crisis. The agencies cut Greece below investment grade in 2010 and early 2011 as the budget deficit was revised up and the first bailout was agreed, then to default when private bondholders took losses in 2012. A second slide came in mid-2015, when talks with creditors broke down and the government imposed capital controls.

The agencies give similar reasons for the upgrades: the fall in public debt from its 2020 peak, steady primary surpluses, reforms and a banking system that no longer threatens the state's finances.914 They also name the same weak points, which are debt that is still high, structural limits on growth and a large current-account deficit.1417

Borrowing almost like Germany

Greece's 10-year government bond yield was 0.78 points above Germany's on average in 2025, against 21 points in 2012.2 Greece has borrowed more cheaply than Italy every month since April 2023, apart from February 2026, when the two were level.1

The spread is the extra interest investors demand to hold Greek government bonds rather than German ones, which the market treats as the safest in the euro area. Because both are in euros, the spread strips out currency risk and leaves mostly the market's view of Greek credit risk. These figures are the EMU convergence yields that the ECB compiles and Eurostat publishes (dataset irt_lt_mcby).

Greece's spread is now below Italy's (1.00 points in 2025) and is close to where it was before the crisis. Through 2003–2007 it was only 0.20–0.31 points.2

Since August 2025 the Greek 10-year yield has also been below France's in 12 of 15 months. In October 2026 Greece paid 4.41%, against 4.68% for Italy and 4.89% for France.1

What this number doesn't say

A small spread is not the same as cheap money. Yields across Europe rose in 2026, and Greece's 10-year yield was 4.41% in October 2026, up from 3.30% a year earlier. Most of that came from Germany, whose yield rose 0.80 points over the same year.1

The spread itself widened in October 2026 to 0.99 points, from 0.62 in January and 0.75 in September. That was the widest since September 2024. One month does not make a trend, and a change of benchmark bond can move the monthly figure, so the November data matter.

Narrow spreads before 2008 also show that markets can misprice risk. In 2007 Greece paid 0.28 points over Germany while its finances were already deteriorating.

29% in 2012: what the crisis did to Greek borrowing costs

At the height of the crisis, in February 2012, the Greek 10-year yield averaged 29.24% for the month, and the spread over Germany reached 27.39 points.1 No government can fund itself at that price, so Greece borrowed from the EU and the IMF instead.

That February was the month of the PSI ("private sector involvement"), in which private holders of Greek government bonds swapped them for new ones and accepted a 53.5% cut in face value.20 The agencies rated Greece in default while it happened.

Yields fell back after the restructuring and the second bailout, then rose again in 2015. They averaged 9.67% that year, with a monthly high of 12.0% in April, before the third bailout was signed.12 From 2016 the trend was downwards, and in 2021 the annual average reached 0.88%, helped by the ECB's pandemic bond purchases.26

Reading the 2012 numbers

These are monthly averages, so the peak on single days was higher. Between 2012 and 2014 there were few or no Greek benchmark bonds trading, so the 2012 figures describe a thin, distorted market more than a price at which Greece could borrow.

What sets the spread now

Today the spread mostly reflects falling debt, the ratings and Greece's unusual debt structure, while the level of yields follows Germany and the ECB.

  • Debt is falling fast. General government debt fell from 209.4% of GDP in 2020 to 145.4% in 2025 (Eurostat).46 The details are on the public finances page. Moody's forecasts 120% by 2030 and Fitch 124.6% by 2028.1517
  • Greece is repaying bailout loans early. Greece repaid €5.3bn of crisis-era loans early at the end of 2025 and plans to repay €13bn more by the end of 2026. Moody's says this cuts gross debt and future debt-servicing needs.15
  • Most of the debt is official, long and cheap. Much of the debt is owed to euro-area governments and the bailout funds on long maturities and low rates, so Greece needs to borrow relatively little from markets each year. Moody's cites a "favourable debt-servicing profile".14
  • Ratings and the investor base. Each upgrade widens the pool of buyers (see above). Moody's positive outlook signals that the next move is more likely up than down.
  • Growth that beats the euro area. The economy grew 2.1% in 2025, against 1.2% for the euro area (Eurostat);47 the detail is on the growth page. Faster nominal growth makes the debt ratio fall even without repayment.

The level of yields is set elsewhere. When German Bund yields rose in 2026 and the ECB raised rates by 0.25 points in June and again in September, Greek yields rose too.143 The Bank of Greece notes that rising government bond yields worldwide are raising refinancing costs for countries with limited fiscal space.29

In plain terms

At the 2025 spread of 0.78 points, every €1bn that Greece borrows for 10 years costs about €7.8m a year more in interest than it would cost Germany. At the 2012 spread of 21 points, the extra cost would have been €210m a year.

Banks cleaned up: bad loans from 46% to 3.4%

Non-performing loans were 3.4% of Greek bank loans in June 2026, according to the Bank of Greece, down from 46.0% in 2017 on the IMF's measure.2928 Most of the fall came from moving bad loans off banks' balance sheets, not from borrowers repaying them.

A loan is non-performing when the borrower is more than 90 days behind on payments or is unlikely to pay in full. At the worst point almost one euro in two lent by Greek banks was in that state, after six years of recession and a quarter of the workforce out of work.

How the stock came down

The stock of non-performing loans peaked in March 2016. By December 2024 it had fallen by €101.2bn, or 94.4%.31 The main tool was the Hellenic Asset Protection Scheme, known as Hercules, launched in December 2019.

  • Under Hercules, banks bundled bad loans into securitisations, sold the riskier slices to investors and kept the safest "senior" notes, which the state guaranteed for a fee.
  • By December 2024 the scheme held €40.7bn of bad loans, with state guarantees of €15.7bn, down from €19.2bn at the start as the notes are repaid.31
  • In 2024 alone the stock fell 39.8% to €6bn, which the Bank of Greece says was "mainly due" to Hercules securitisations and secondarily to loan sales. Recovery through selling collateral was "negligible".31

The ratio fell to 3.3% in December 2025, the lowest since Greece joined the euro area, and then edged up to 3.4% in June 2026 as the stock rose 9.3% to €6.2bn.3029

What this number doesn't say

The loans still exist. Hercules and the loan sales moved them from banks to funds and the credit servicers that manage loans for them. In June 2026 servicers managed €91.3bn of exposures, and 84.7% of them were non-performing.29 Recoveries have lagged the business plans, and the Bank of Greece has flagged the risk that the state guarantees could cost taxpayers money.3344

Greece is still about double the EU average. EU banks' average was 1.8% in September 2025.32

New bad loans are rising. €1.7bn of performing loans turned bad in the first half of 2026, while €388m recovered.34 The ratio is 8.6% for households and 11.2% for very small businesses.4334

Definitions have also changed over time, and the IMF series (3.0% in 2025) differs from the Bank of Greece's figure (3.3%) because it is compiled on a different basis.

The four big banks: from bailouts to dividends

Greece's four systemic banks, National Bank of Greece, Eurobank, Alpha Bank and Piraeus, were rescued with public money in the crisis. They returned to private hands in 2023–2024 and paid their first dividends since 2008 in 2024.293742

The rescue

The state set up the Hellenic Financial Stability Fund (HFSF) in July 2010, funded with bailout money, to recapitalise the banks in return for shares.40 In spring 2013 the HFSF and the Bank of Greece merged ten banks into four. The fund recapitalised them that year and again after the 2015 crisis, when it put €2.72bn into Piraeus alone.4045

The exit

Once Greece regained investment grade, the HFSF sold its stakes within a year.

The sales were possible because bank shares had recovered, a large part of the Athens stock market's rebound.

Profits, dividends and capital

In June 2024 the ECB, which supervises the four banks, allowed them to pay dividends for the first time since 2008. They paid about €814m out of 2023 profits.4142 Banking groups earned €4.7bn after tax in 2025, up from €4.2bn in 2024, and €2.6bn in the first half of 2026, a return on equity of 12.1%.3029

The core capital ratio (CET1) was 14.8% in June 2026 and the total capital ratio 19.6%.29

The capital catch

Over a third of Greek banks' core capital, 36.0% in June 2026, is deferred tax credits: tax assets built up in the crisis years that the state has guaranteed to honour. That share is falling (39.7% in December 2025), but it is lower-quality capital than retained profits.29

Lending is growing again

Loans to businesses grew 9.2% in the year to September 2026, more than twice the euro-area rate of 4.3%, on the ECB's measure adjusted for loan sales and securitisations.35 Loans to households shrank year on year in every month from November 2010 to April 2025, and have grown since May 2025, by 2.65% in the year to September 2026.

Growth is slowing from the 2024 peak. On the Bank of Greece's own measure, credit to businesses grew 7.8% in September 2026, down from 9.6% in August, and the average rate on new business loans rose to 4.5% in August 2026 from 3.7% in December 2025.3643

What to watch: the road to an A rating

The next milestone is a return to the A range, which Greece last held in June 2010. Scope is one notch away, the others two or three.

  • Moody's. The positive outlook since 18 Sep 2026 means Moody's could raise Greece to Baa2, level with the BBB of the other agencies, if fiscal gains hold and debt keeps falling. Moody's says its confidence that the fiscal gains will last is growing "but not yet fully established".1415
  • The A range. A− needs BBB+ first at S&P, Fitch and DBRS. The agencies tie it to the debt path, continued surpluses and political stability.44
  • The spread. Whether October's 0.99 points marks a turn or a blip will show in the November and December data.
  • New bad loans. The rise in defaults among households and very small businesses in the first half of 2026, and the government's new framework for private debt and credit servicers, put out for consultation in September 2026.29
  • Credit growth. Whether business lending keeps growing as loan rates rise, and whether households keep borrowing after almost 15 years in which their bank debt shrank.
  • Hercules guarantees. Whether recoveries on the securitised loans are enough to keep the state guarantees from being called.
The takeaway

Greek credit has been repaired in the sense that matters to investors: mainstream funds can buy Greek bonds, banks are profitable and private again, and the state pays less than Italy to borrow. The legacy is not gone, though. It has moved to servicers' books, deferred tax credits and a debt ratio that is still one of the highest in the EU.
