Greece is growing faster than the euro area, but has not yet won back 2008

Greece's real GDP grew 2.1% in 2025 against 1.2% for the euro area, according to Eurostat, the fifth year in a row it grew faster. Household spending, investment backed by EU recovery funds, and exports did most of the work. The economy is still 13.9% smaller than in 2008, and GDP per head is 69.6% of the EU average. The European Commission expects growth to slow to 1.5% in 2027 as recovery money ends.

Growing faster than the euro area

Greece's economy has grown faster than the euro area's every year since 2021: by 8.7% against 6.5% in 2021, 5.5% against 3.7% in 2022, 2.0% against 0.4% in 2023, 2.1% against 1.3% in 2024 and 2.1% against 1.2% in 2025.1

These are changes in real GDP, the value of everything the economy produces with inflation stripped out. Eurostat publishes them from figures supplied by ELSTAT, the Greek statistics office, and still flags 2024 and 2025 as provisional, so they can be revised.1

The lead has held into 2026. In the second quarter of 2026, Greek output was 1.9% higher than a year earlier, against 1.2% for the euro area.2 Since 2018, Greece has grown more slowly than the euro area only in 2020, when the pandemic hit its tourism-heavy economy harder (−9.2% against −6.0%).

What this number doesn't say

Faster growth is partly the arithmetic of catching up. An economy that lost a quarter of its output has more slack to take up, so growing faster than the euro area is what you would expect in a recovery. The level of output, in the next section, is the tougher test.

The economy is still about 14% smaller than in 2008

Real GDP in 2025 was 13.9% below its 2008 peak, while the euro area's was 17.7% above its own 2008 level.1

Between 2008 and 2013 Greece lost 27.0% of its real output, more than a quarter. It then barely grew for four years, fell again by 9.2% in the pandemic year of 2020, and has recovered 18.0% from the 2013 low.

How the crisis unfolded

  • 2008Real GDP peaks. The global financial crisis exposes a large budget deficit and a debt that markets stop financing.
  • 2010The first of three EU and IMF rescue programmes begins. Output falls 5.7%.
  • 2011The worst year: real GDP falls 9.9%.
  • 2013The low point, 27.0% below 2008.
  • Aug 2015The third programme, from the European Stability Mechanism, is agreed. The economy is flat in 2015 and 2016.
  • 20 Aug 2018Greece exits its last programme. Across the three, lenders disbursed €288.7bn.16
  • 2020The pandemic: output falls 9.2%, to 70.6% of the 2008 level.
  • 2025Five years of recovery bring output to 86.1% of 2008.
In plain terms

Per person the gap is smaller, because there are fewer people. Real GDP per head was €19,700 in 2025 against €21,420 in 2008, a fall of 8.0%, while the average population fell from 11.08 million to 10.37 million.510 Part of that fall is the emigration of the crisis years, covered on our brain drain and brain regain page.

How long until 2008?

Output needs to rise another 16.1% to regain its 2008 level. On the IMF's October 2026 projections of 1.6–1.8% a year, it would still be about 5% short in 2031, so on that path the old peak returns in the mid-2030s.12 That date is our arithmetic, not an IMF forecast.

What has driven growth since 2021?

Household spending did the most, with investment and exports close behind, but most of the extra demand was met by imports, which took back about as much growth as exports added.3

Eurostat splits each year's growth into contributions, in percentage points, from each part of spending. Adding up 2021 to 2025, real GDP growth totals 20.5 points: household spending contributed 14.3, exports 11.8, investment 10.1 and government spending 1.0. The four engines

  • Consumers. Household spending added 6.3 points in 2022 as the economy reopened, then between 1.2 and 1.7 points a year. It rose alongside falling unemployment, but real household spending in 2025 was still 8.3% below 2008.4
  • Investment and the Recovery Fund. Investment added between 1.2 and 3.3 points every year. Much of it was paid for by the EU's Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF), the pandemic-era fund under which Greece was allocated €18.22bn in grants and €17.73bn in loans.14
  • Tourism. Services make up just over half of Greek exports, and travel is the largest part. Tourism receipts reached a record €23.6bn in 2025 (Bank of Greece, provisional), equal to about 9.5% of nominal GDP of €249.7bn.171
  • Exports of goods. Exports added 7.7 points in 2021 and 2.5 in 2022 but only 0.2 in 2025, so their push has faded (see exports).
What this breakdown doesn't say

Imports show up as a minus because GDP counts only what is produced at home, and much of what Greeks buy and invest in, from cars to machinery, is made abroad. The inventories line swings from year to year and is partly a statistical balancing item, so read it lightly. The totals are simple sums of annual contributions, not compound growth.

GDP per head is 69.6% of the EU average, the second lowest in the EU

Adjusted for price levels, Greece's GDP per head was 69.6% of the EU average in 2025, up from a low of 62.4% in 2020 but far below the 93.0% of 2008.5

This measure uses purchasing power standards (PPS), an artificial currency that corrects for the fact that a euro buys more in Athens than in Amsterdam. It is the standard way to compare living standards across the EU.

Only Bulgaria, at 68.1%, was lower in 2025. Greece was overtaken during the crisis years by Portugal, the Baltic states, Poland, Romania, Croatia, Hungary and Slovakia. The recovery since 2020 has closed 7.2 points of the gap, but the ratio has hardly moved since 2023 (69.5%, 69.4%, 69.6%).5

Two versions of the same number

Eurostat's separate purchasing-power table (tec00114), which runs on a different publication schedule, rounds Greece to 68 for 2023–2025.6 We use the national-accounts table updated in November 2026. Either way the picture is the same: Greece is close to the bottom of the EU and has recovered less than half of the ground it lost.

Investment has nearly closed the gap with the euro area, but is still in the EU's bottom three

Investment rose to 18.0% of GDP in 2025 from 11.0% in 2019, narrowing the gap with the euro area (21.3%) from 10.9 points to 3.3.1

The measure is gross fixed capital formation: spending on buildings, infrastructure, machinery, vehicles and software. For a decade after the crisis Greece invested barely half the euro-area rate, which wore down its capital stock. In real terms, investment in 2025 was 95.0% higher than in 2019, but still 31.4% below 2007.4

Three things helped: RRF money, banks that have cleared most of their bad loans, and foreign investors, who put a record €12.55bn of direct investment into Greece in 2025.

The catch

Only Luxembourg (16.2%) and Poland (17.3%) invested a smaller share of GDP in 2025. Part of the rise in current euros is inflation, and a large share of recent investment depended on RRF money that stops in 2026.

Exports have nearly doubled as a share of GDP since 2010, but have slipped since 2022

Exports of goods and services were 39.3% of GDP in 2025, up from 21.7% in 2010, but down from a peak of 49.2% in 2022.1

Greece used to be one of the most closed economies in the euro area. The crisis pushed firms to sell abroad, and in real terms exports were 57.9% higher in 2025 than in 2010.4

The fall since 2022 is mostly about prices. The 2022 peak was swollen by high energy prices, which raised the value of Greece's refined-fuel exports: goods exports fell from €54.2bn in 2022 to €47.4bn in 2025, while services rose to €50.7bn. In real terms exports have still grown since 2022, but by only 3.6% over three years.14

What this number doesn't say

Greece is still below the euro-area average of 48.6%, although that average is pulled up by small, very open economies such as Ireland and Belgium.

Manufacturing output is up 16% since 2021, while the euro area's is flat

Greek manufacturing output rose 16.1% between 2021 and 2025, while euro-area manufacturing was 0.3% lower.7

The index measures the volume of factory output, not its value. Greek output kept rising into 2026, reaching 120.3 in September (2021 = 100, seasonally adjusted).8

The catch

The rise starts from a low base. Greek manufacturing fell 13% between 2010 and 2013 and took until 2021 to recover, so over the full 2010–2025 period it is up 23%.

Productivity is the missing piece of the recovery

Output per hour worked in Greece was 54.1% of the EU average in 2025, adjusted for price levels, down from 76.9% in 2008.9

The recovery since 2013 has come mostly from putting people back to work, not from each hour of work producing more. The employment rate is at a record, but on Eurostat's measure real output per hour in 2025 was 14.9% below 2008 and only 1.8% above 2019. The EU average rose 3.4% over 2019–2025.9

This is the main reason the gap in income per head closes so slowly. Greek workers put in about 1,981 hours each in 2025, against 1,605 for the EU average and the second most in the EU after Poland, so low output per hour shows up less in GDP per head (69.6%) than in GDP per hour (54.1%).9

How solid is this?

Productivity numbers depend on counts of hours worked, which are hard to measure, especially in a country with many self-employed people and a sizeable informal economy. The level comparison with the EU is approximate, but the trend is clear across Eurostat's measures.

Forecasts: growth is expected to slow to about 1.5–1.8% from 2027

The European Commission forecasts Greek growth of 1.8% in 2026 and 1.5% in 2027 and 2028, still faster than its forecast for the euro area (1.2%, 1.2% and 1.3%).11

The IMF is a little more optimistic, at 1.8% for 2026 and 2027 and 1.7% for 2028, and the Greek government's draft 2027 budget is the most optimistic, at 2.0% for 2026 and 2.3% for 2027. The government expects investment to rise 7.9% and private consumption 1.5% in 2027, according to Reuters.1213 All of these are forecasts.

Why growth is expected to slow

  • The Recovery Fund ends. Under EU rules, RRF reforms and investments had to be completed by 31 August 2026 and the last payments made by 31 December 2026.15 The Commission expects record RRF inflows in 2026, then slower growth in 2027 and 2028 "as RRF spending winds down".11
  • Energy prices. A new energy-price shock linked to the war in the Middle East led the Bank of Greece to cut its 2026 forecast from 2.1% to 1.9% in April 2026.18 The Commission says the shock is reducing household real incomes and expects inflation to peak at 4.1% in 2026.11
  • Tighter financing. The Commission also cites higher energy costs and tighter financing conditions as a drag on investment.11
What to watch

Whether private investment and foreign capital replace RRF money after 2026; whether energy prices settle; the third-quarter 2026 GDP figures, which ELSTAT has not yet published; and whether the budget surplus survives the tax cuts and new benefits put to parliament in November,20 which matters for debt and for the country's credit ratings. For a market view, see Morgan Stanley's latest read on the outlook (Greek Reporter, 16 Nov 2026).19

Forecasts are not results

Forecasts for Greece have often been revised, and this year's were cut in spring when energy prices jumped. The government's 2.3% for 2027 is well above the Commission's 1.5%, and its forecast rests on investment growing 7.9% in a year when RRF money will have stopped.
