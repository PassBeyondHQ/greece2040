Greek unemployment has fallen by two thirds since 2013

Greece's unemployment rate fell from 27.8% in 2013 to 8.9% in 2025, and the monthly rate reached 7.4% in August 2026, the lowest since May 2008 (Eurostat). A record 71.0% of 20–64 year-olds are in work, and the minimum wage is €920 a month.

Unemployment is down by two thirds since 2013

Greece's unemployment rate averaged 27.8% in 2013 and 8.9% in 2025, a fall of 18.9 percentage points, so roughly two in three of the jobless of 2013 are no longer counted as unemployed.1

The rate counts people aged 15–74 who have no job and are actively looking, as a share of everyone working or looking (the labour force). It comes from the Labour Force Survey run by ELSTAT, the Greek statistics office.

The fall was slow at first and then steady. The rate was 25.0% in 2015, the year of capital controls, and 17.6% in 2020, the pandemic year, barely changed from 17.9% in 2019. It has fallen by about one to three points every year since, as the economy grew faster than the euro area.

The gap with the euro area tells the same story from another angle. In 2013 Greek unemployment was 15.6 points above the euro-area rate of 12.2%. In 2025 the gap was 2.5 points (8.9% against 6.4%).1 On the latest monthly data it is about one point: 7.6% in September 2026 against 6.5% for the euro area.2

In plain terms

In September 2026 ELSTAT counted 359,609 unemployed people in Greece, 57,627 fewer than a year earlier, and 4,391,208 people in work.34

August 2026 brought the lowest monthly rate since May 2008

Seasonally adjusted unemployment was 7.4% in August 2026, the lowest monthly reading since May 2008, when it was 7.3%.2

The monthly peak was 28.3% in July 2013. September 2026 came in at 7.6%, against 8.8% a year earlier.24 "Seasonally adjusted" means the statisticians strip out the regular summer hiring for tourism, so one month can be compared with the next.

Treat any single month with care. The Greek monthly series is volatile: it moved from 8.1% in December 2025 to 10.3% in March 2026 and back to 7.8% by May, swings that the annual figures do not show.2 Monthly figures are also revised as later survey data and new seasonal factors arrive.

How to read the monthly rate

The trend is clearer in the annual averages: 10.1% in 2024 and 8.9% in 2025. A one-month low of 7.4% shows the direction, but it is the 2026 annual average that will confirm the level. The European Commission forecasts 8.4% for 2026 as a whole, and the IMF 8.0%.2223

Long-term unemployment is down from 17.5% to 5.0%

Long-term unemployment, meaning people who have been looking for work for 12 months or more, fell from 17.5% of the labour force in 2014 to 5.0% in 2025.5

A record share of adults is in work

In 2025, 71.0% of Greeks aged 20–64 had a job, up from 52.5% in 2013 and the highest in Eurostat's records, which go back to 1995. The previous high was 66.3% in 2008.67

The employment rate is the share of a whole age group that is in work, so unlike the unemployment rate it is not flattered when people stop looking for a job. The latest quarterly figure is higher still: 72.2% in the second quarter of 2026, seasonally adjusted.8

About 4.18 million people aged 20–64 were in work in 2025, 751,000 more than at the 2013 low.6

Women account for most of the gain in jobs

The employment rate of women aged 20–64 rose from 42.9% in 2013 to 62.3% in 2025, a gain of 19.4 points, and there are now more women in work than in 2009.6

About 1.83 million women aged 20–64 had a job in 2025, against 1.78 million in 2009.6

Youth unemployment has fallen from 59.2% to 19.1%

Unemployment among 15–24 year-olds averaged 19.1% in 2025, down from 59.2% in 2013.1

The youth rate is measured against young people in the labour force only. Most 15–24 year-olds are students and are not counted, so 19.1% does not mean one young Greek in five is jobless. It means that among those who want a job and are looking, about one in five has none.

The monthly figure for September 2026 was 17.3%, down from 20% a year earlier.4

NEETs: young people neither working nor studying

A wider measure is the NEET rate: the share of all 15–29 year-olds who are not in employment, education or training. It fell from 28.5% in 2013 to 13.6% in 2025, against an EU average of 11.0%.10

The minimum wage is €920, up from €586 after the 2012 cut

Since 1 April 2026 the statutory minimum wage in Greece has been €920 a month gross, up from €586.08 after the 2012 cut. It is paid 14 times a year, as Greek salaries are.1213

In February 2012, under the second bailout programme, the minimum was cut by 22%, from €751.39 to €586.08, and it stayed frozen for seven years. Since February 2019 it has been raised seven times, and every rise since 2023 has taken effect in April.1113

What €920 buys: the real-terms view

These are nominal figures, before inflation. Deflated with Eurostat's consumer price index (HICP), €920 in April 2026 buys about 24% more than €586.08 did when the cut took effect, but about 1.8% less than the €751.39 of January 2012.14

Comparing with other countries

Because most countries pay 12 salaries a year, Eurostat spreads the Greek 14 payments over 12 months. On that basis the Greek minimum wage was €1,073 a month from July 2026.11

Job vacancies have more than tripled since 2019

With unemployment down, job vacancies averaged 41,912 in 2025, more than three times the 12,644 of 2019, and construction and tourism are both adding workers.18

Construction

The sector lost almost two thirds of its jobs between 2008 and 2015, and it is now rebuilding with a pipeline of public works such as the E65 motorway and large private schemes like the Ellinikon. Construction employment rose from 143,600 in 2015 to 196,700 in 2025.17

Hotels and tourism

Accommodation and food services employed 391,300 people in 2025, more than in any year since at least 2008, on the back of record tourism receipts.17

What to watch

The points that will shape the Greek labour market from here.

  • Pay. Average full-time pay was €18,772 in 2025, against €20,225 in 2008 and an EU average of €41,464. It has risen 18.4% since 2020, and in real terms it is about 29% below 2008 and 2.4% below 2020.1514
  • Productivity. Greece produced 54.1% of the EU average value per hour worked in 2025 (76.9% in 2008), while the average worker put in 1,981 hours against 1,605 across the EU.16
  • The minimum wage. The April 2026 rise to €920 kept pace with inflation, which reached 5.7% in October 2026. The April 2027 decision comes against the government's €950 target.1314
  • The headcount. 4.18 million people aged 20–64 were in work in 2025, 244,000 fewer than the 4.42 million of 2009, as the working-age population fell by about 876,000. Men account for the shortfall, about 300,000, much of it in construction (394,700 jobs in 2008, 196,700 in 2025).6917
  • The EU gap. The employment rate of 71.0% is 5.1 points below the EU average of 76.1%, and the gap between men's and women's employment is 17.4 points against 9.6 in the EU.6
  • Long-term unemployment. It was 5.0% of the labour force in 2025, against an EU average of 1.9%, and 55.8% of the unemployed had been looking for a year or more, against 31.5% in the EU.5
  • The 2026 unemployment rate, due from Eurostat in spring 2027. The European Commission forecasts 8.4% for 2026, 8.0% for 2027 and 7.7% for 2028, and the IMF 8.0% and 7.6% for 2026 and 2027.2223
  • New rules at work. In November 2026 the labour ministry tabled Greece's first law on AI and algorithmic management at work.24
