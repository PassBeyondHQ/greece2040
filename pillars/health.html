Greeks live longer than the EU average, and the health system behind them is under repair

Life expectancy at birth in Greece reached a record 81.9 years in 2024, above the EU's 81.5 (Eurostat), and avoidable deaths are below the EU rate. The system is weaker than the longevity suggests: households pay about 34% of health costs out of pocket, more than double the EU share, and 11.5% of adults reported unmet medical need in 2025, about five times the EU rate.

Greeks now live longer than the average European

A baby born in Greece in 2024 could expect to live 81.9 years, the highest figure ever recorded for the country and 0.4 years more than the EU average of 81.5.1

Life expectancy at birth is not a forecast for any one person. It is the average number of years a newborn would live if the death rates of that year held for the rest of its life, which makes it a snapshot of how healthy a country is right now. Eurostat calculates it from national death and population records, so the figures are comparable across the EU.1

The COVID dip, and the recovery

The pandemic took 1.3 years off Greek life expectancy, from 81.4 in 2019 to 80.1 in 2021, a slightly deeper fall than the EU's drop from 81.1 to 80.0. Greece then recovered faster: it passed its pre-pandemic level in 2023 (81.7) and set a new record in 2024.1

Women live longer than men, as everywhere in Europe. In 2024 Greek women could expect 84.4 years and men 79.4, both above the EU averages of 84.1 and 78.9.1 At 65, a Greek could expect another 20.3 years, against 20.2 in the EU.1

What this number doesn't say

Greece's lead over the EU is shrinking. It was 1.2 years in 2005 and 0.4 years in 2024, because the EU gained 3.1 years over that period and Greece 2.3. Longer lives are not all healthy ones either: of the 20.3 years a 65-year-old Greek can expect, Eurostat counts 10.1 as healthy life years, free of limitations in daily activities, against 10.3 in the EU.3

Above the EU average, but behind its Mediterranean neighbours

Greece ranked 16th of the 27 EU countries for life expectancy in 2024, level with the Netherlands, and behind Spain, Italy, Cyprus, Malta and Portugal.1

The comparison that flatters Greece is the EU average, which is pulled down by central and eastern European countries, many of which still have life expectancy below 80. The fairer one is with the rest of southern Europe, which shares much of Greece's climate and diet: Spain leads the EU at 84.0 years, and Italy (83.7) and Cyprus (83.5) are close behind.1 The 2025 OECD and EU country profile reaches the same conclusion, describing Greece as slightly above the EU average but below most southern and western European countries.2

Fewer Greeks die of causes that could have been prevented or treated

Greece recorded 221 avoidable deaths per 100,000 people in 2023, below the EU's 238 and the lowest figure since the series began in 2011.4

Avoidable mortality counts deaths before the age of 75 that should not happen in a country with good public health and good medical care. It has two halves. Preventable deaths are those that public health and prevention could largely avoid, such as lung cancer from smoking. Treatable deaths are those that timely, effective healthcare could avoid, such as many heart attacks and early-stage cancers. Rates are age-standardised, so countries with older populations are not penalised.4

COVID-19 pushed the rate to 290 in 2021, and most of that jump was in preventable deaths, which rose by 42% between 2019 and 2021, according to the OECD and EU profile.2 By 2023 both halves were below their pre-pandemic level. Even so, Greece ranks 14th lowest of the 27 EU countries, behind Cyprus (153), Italy (160) and Spain (171).4

The biggest single risk is tobacco. One Greek adult in four smoked daily in 2019, among the highest rates in the EU, and lung cancer causes about a quarter of all cancer deaths. Cardiovascular disease caused almost a third of all deaths in 2022.2

What the diet, and Ikaria, can and can't explain

The traditional Greek diet and way of life are part of the longevity story, and the island of Ikaria is studied as one of the world's longevity "blue zones", but the national data show younger Greeks drifting away from those habits.

In 2013 UNESCO added the Mediterranean diet to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity, naming Greece alongside Cyprus, Croatia, Italy, Morocco, Portugal and Spain.5 Some of the protective habits still show up in the data. Greek adults drink 6.6 litres of pure alcohol a year, among the lowest in the EU (average 9.8 litres), more than half are physically active more than three days a week, and adult obesity is 12% against an EU average of 15%.2

Ikaria, the "blue zone"

Ikaria, an island of the north-eastern Aegean, is one of four areas the demographers Michel Poulain, Anne Herm and Gianni Pes studied in 2013 as "blue zones": limited regions where people share a lifestyle and environment and where exceptional longevity has been accurately verified. The others are Sardinia's Ogliastra, Okinawa and Costa Rica's Nicoya peninsula.6

The Ikaria Study, led by Demosthenes Panagiotakos and colleagues, enrolled 1,420 residents aged 30 and over in 2009, of whom 187 were over 80. It found a share of people over 90 much higher than the European average, a high score for Mediterranean-diet adherence (38 out of 55 on the MedDietScore, against 33 in an earlier study of older people on other Mediterranean islands), daily physical activity well into old age and a habit of midday naps.7

What Ikaria doesn't prove

The Ikaria Study was a volunteer sample at one point in time, so it shows associations, not causes, and its authors say so. A single island says little about national life expectancy, which depends far more on smoking, heart disease and access to care on the mainland.

The warning sign

The habits are fading among the young. In 2022, 28% of Greek 15-year-olds were overweight or obese, up from 22% in 2018 and above the EU average of 21%, and only 21% ate a portion of fruit every day. A National Action Plan against Childhood Obesity started in November 2023.2

Plenty of doctors, too few nurses

Greece reports more doctors per person than any other EU country, 6.6 per 1,000 people in 2023 against an EU average of 4.3, but only 3.8 nurses per 1,000, less than half the EU's 8.5.2, 8

Both numbers need care. The Greek doctor count includes everyone licensed to practise, not only those working, so it overstates the real workforce. The nurse count covers only nurses employed in public hospitals, so it understates it.2 The imbalance is real all the same. In Eurostat's comparison of practising nurses, Greece's 338 per 100,000 people (2019, the latest year reported) is the lowest of the 22 EU countries that report the figure.8

Too few family doctors as well

Greece trains specialists, not generalists. General practitioners made up about 6% of doctors in 2023, the lowest share in the EU, where the average is 19%. GP density did double between 2010 and 2023, from 0.19 to 0.38 per 1,000 people, as public primary care expanded.2

The nursing gap is not for lack of graduates. Greece produced 100.6 nursing graduates per 100,000 people in 2023, more than twice the EU average of 38.6, but the public system has not absorbed them, and there is no formal career path to senior nursing roles.2 Keeping trained people in the country is the same problem the brain-drain pillar tracks for the wider economy.

Greece spends less on health than most of the EU

Current health spending in Greece was 8.4% of GDP in 2024, against 10.2% for the EU, and about 56% of the EU level per person once prices are adjusted.9

Health spending was one of the casualties of the debt crisis and the bailout programmes. It fell from 9.6% of GDP in 2010 to 7.9% in 2014, while GDP itself was shrinking, and spending per person in purchasing power terms fell by about a quarter over the same years.9 The story of that period is told in the public debt pillar.

Since then spending per person has risen in almost every year, to 2,291 in purchasing power standards (a common unit that strips out price differences) in 2024, against 4,075 in the EU.9 The share of GDP has edged down since 2021 partly because the economy has been growing faster than health budgets. Public spending per person grew 26% in real terms between 2015 and 2023, slightly faster than the EU's 24%, and health has taken a steady 11.3–11.9% of all government spending since 2019.2

In plain terms

The state pays for only 61% of Greek health spending, the second-lowest share in the EU, against about 80% in the EU as a whole.2, 9 The rest falls mostly on patients, which is the subject of the next section.

Households pay a third of the bill themselves

Out-of-pocket payments, money patients pay directly at the point of care, covered 34.2% of Greek health spending in 2024, more than double the EU share of 15.1% and the highest in the EU in the latest data.9

This is not a crisis leftover that is fading. The share was 37.2% in 2014 and has stayed between 33% and 37% every year since.9 According to the OECD and EU profile, the money goes mainly on medicines (37% of out-of-pocket spending in 2023) and hospital care (34%, by far the highest such share in the EU). Public cover for dentistry is so thin that in practice all dental care is paid privately.2

  • Retail medicines take 27% of all Greek health spending, double the EU average of 13%, and public schemes cover only 52% of that bill.2
  • There is no annual ceiling on co-payments, the fixed shares patients pay for tests and medicines.2
  • In 2023, 9.5% of Greek households faced catastrophic health spending, meaning health costs above 40% of what they had left after basic needs such as food and housing, against 6.4% on average in the EU.2

One adult in nine says they went without care they needed

In 2025, 11.5% of Greeks aged 16 and over said they had needed a medical examination in the past year and not had one because it was too expensive, too far away or the waiting list was too long. That is the highest rate in the EU and about five times the EU average of 2.4%.10

The figure comes from EU-SILC, the EU's household survey on income and living conditions. In Greece it more than doubled during the crisis, to 13.1% in 2016, and then halved to 6.4% by 2021, years in which unemployment was falling. Since 2022 it has climbed back, to 12.1% in 2024 and 11.5% in 2025. The next highest country in 2025 was Finland, at 7.8%.10

Cost is the main reason. On its own, "too expensive" accounted for 8.8% of Greek adults in 2025, against 1.0% in the EU, while waiting lists accounted for 2.0% (EU 1.2%) and distance 0.7% (EU 0.1%).10 That links this number directly to the out-of-pocket share above, and it hits the poorest hardest.

Two versions of this number

You may also see 21.9% quoted for Greece. That is the OECD and EU profile's figure for 2024, and it uses a smaller base: only the people who said they needed care, rather than all adults. On that measure Greece is six times the EU average of 3.6%, and 32.3% of people at risk of poverty who needed care went without it.2

What is being repaired, and what to watch

Greece is putting €1.5bn from the EU Recovery and Resilience Facility into health, 4.3% of its total allocation, alongside new screening programmes, a personal-doctor system, digital records and three donor-funded hospitals.2

The Recovery Fund money pays mainly for buildings and IT. The European Commission lists a €254m project for interventions in 80 public hospitals in all seven health regions, and a €199m project to upgrade 156 health centres.11, 12 In July 2026 Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis said more than 150 health centres had been fully upgraded and more than 80 hospitals had received major work, mostly to emergency departments, and in August the finance ministry put the number of completed renovations of hospitals and health centres at more than 230.13, 14 These are government figures, and the definitions differ from one statement to the next.

  • 2022The "personal doctor" system makes a GP or internist the first point of contact for every adult. It is widened in 2024 to private and rural doctors.2
  • Nov 2023Construction starts at Komotini, the first of three new public hospitals funded by the Stavros Niarchos Foundation (SNF) and designed by Renzo Piano, with Sparta and a children's hospital in Thessaloniki to follow.15, 16
  • 2024The "Prolamvano" ("I prevent") screening plan offers free breast, cervical and colorectal cancer screening and heart checks, funded by the Recovery Fund. A single digital waiting list for surgery in state hospitals also starts.2
  • May 2025A fully operational electronic health record goes live: patients can see diagnoses, prescriptions, test results and hospital records through a central portal or the MyHealth app, where e-prescriptions also arrive.2
  • Jun 2025Adults who had not chosen a personal doctor are enrolled automatically. In May the health ministry counted 5,115,264 people registered and 5,496 personal doctors.17
  • May 2026The government says Prolamvano has reached more than 6 million people, including about 1.21m digital mammograms, and commits €300m to extend it to 2030.18
  • End-2027SNF's target to complete and hand over all three hospitals, later than the dates first announced. The three cost more than €500m.15

Screening matters because Greece starts from a low base for some cancers: in 2019 only 10% of people aged 50–74 had been screened for colorectal cancer in the previous two years, against 33% in the EU, although breast and cervical screening rates were above the EU average.2 The new SNF hospitals are among the larger public projects in the country and are tracked with the others in the infrastructure pillar; they will also carry rooftop solar panels, part of the wider energy transition.15 The digital side, from e-prescriptions to the health record, draws on the same state digitalisation drive covered in the tech pillar, and 38% of Greeks accessed their health information online in 2024, against 28% in the EU.2

What buildings don't fix

New wards need staff, and SNF itself says staffing of its hospitals depends on commitments made by the Greek state.15 So far the reforms have not moved the two weakest numbers on this page, out-of-pocket costs and unmet need. A 2024 scheme that lets patients pay for afternoon surgery in public hospitals and skip the queue, meant to clear the post-pandemic backlog, has raised concerns about equity and about capacity, the OECD and EU profile notes, given shortages of anaesthetists and nurses.2

What to watch

  • Unmet need in the 2026 EU-SILC survey, published by Eurostat in 2027: the clearest test of whether the reforms reach the people who now go without care.
  • The out-of-pocket share in Eurostat's 2025 health accounts, which has not moved below 33% in a decade.
  • Nurse hiring: whether the public system starts to absorb the nurses Greece already trains.
  • The SNF hospitals' handover at the end of 2027, and whether they open fully staffed.
  • Funding after the Recovery Fund: Greece aims to absorb its full €36bn allocation by the end of 2026, so the running costs of the new facilities fall to the national budget, for which the draft 2027 budget forecasts a primary surplus (a surplus before interest payments) of 3.3% of GDP.14, 19
