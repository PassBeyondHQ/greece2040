Greeks live longer than the EU average

Life expectancy at birth in Greece reached a record 81.9 years in 2024, above the EU's 81.5 (Eurostat), and avoidable deaths are below the EU rate. The health system is being modernised: free cancer and heart screening has reached more than 6 million people, the government says, every adult is enrolled with a personal doctor, and €1.5bn of EU recovery money is going into health.

Greeks now live longer than the average European

A baby born in Greece in 2024 could expect to live 81.9 years, the highest figure ever recorded for the country and 0.4 years more than the EU average of 81.5.1

Life expectancy at birth is not a forecast for any one person. It is the average number of years a newborn would live if the death rates of that year held for the rest of its life, which makes it a snapshot of how healthy a country is right now. Eurostat calculates it from national death and population records, so the figures are comparable across the EU.1

The COVID dip, and the recovery

The pandemic took 1.3 years off Greek life expectancy, from 81.4 in 2019 to 80.1 in 2021, a slightly deeper fall than the EU's drop from 81.1 to 80.0. Greece then recovered faster: it passed its pre-pandemic level in 2023 (81.7) and set a new record in 2024.1

Women live longer than men, as everywhere in Europe. In 2024 Greek women could expect 84.4 years and men 79.4, both above the EU averages of 84.1 and 78.9.1 At 65, a Greek could expect another 20.3 years, against 20.2 in the EU.1

Fewer Greeks die of causes that could have been prevented or treated

Greece recorded 221 avoidable deaths per 100,000 people in 2023, below the EU's 238 and the lowest figure since the series began in 2011.4

Avoidable mortality counts deaths before the age of 75 that should not happen in a country with good public health and good medical care. It has two halves. Preventable deaths are those that public health and prevention could largely avoid, such as lung cancer from smoking. Treatable deaths are those that timely, effective healthcare could avoid, such as many heart attacks and early-stage cancers. Rates are age-standardised, so countries with older populations are not penalised.4

COVID-19 pushed the rate to 290 in 2021, and most of that jump was in preventable deaths, which rose by 42% between 2019 and 2021, according to the OECD and EU profile.2 By 2023 both halves were below their pre-pandemic level.4

The biggest single risk is tobacco. One Greek adult in four smoked daily in 2019, among the highest rates in the EU, and lung cancer causes about a quarter of all cancer deaths. Cardiovascular disease caused almost a third of all deaths in 2022.2

The Mediterranean diet and Ikaria

The traditional Greek diet and way of life are part of the longevity story, and the island of Ikaria is studied as one of the world's longevity "blue zones".

In 2013 UNESCO added the Mediterranean diet to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity, naming Greece alongside Cyprus, Croatia, Italy, Morocco, Portugal and Spain.5 Some of the protective habits still show up in the data. Greek adults drink 6.6 litres of pure alcohol a year, among the lowest in the EU (average 9.8 litres), more than half are physically active more than three days a week, and adult obesity is 12% against an EU average of 15%.2

Ikaria, the "blue zone"

Ikaria, an island of the north-eastern Aegean, is one of four areas the demographers Michel Poulain, Anne Herm and Gianni Pes studied in 2013 as "blue zones": limited regions where people share a lifestyle and environment and where exceptional longevity has been accurately verified. The others are Sardinia's Ogliastra, Okinawa and Costa Rica's Nicoya peninsula.6

The Ikaria Study, led by Demosthenes Panagiotakos and colleagues, enrolled 1,420 residents aged 30 and over in 2009, of whom 187 were over 80. It found a share of people over 90 much higher than the European average, a high score for Mediterranean-diet adherence (38 out of 55 on the MedDietScore, against 33 in an earlier study of older people on other Mediterranean islands), daily physical activity well into old age and a habit of midday naps.7

What Ikaria doesn't prove

The Ikaria Study was a volunteer sample at one point in time, so it shows associations, not causes, and its authors say so.

Greece has the most doctors per person in the EU

Greece reports more doctors per person than any other EU country, 6.6 per 1,000 people in 2023 against an EU average of 4.3.2, 8

Both numbers need care. The Greek doctor count includes everyone licensed to practise, not only those working, so it overstates the real workforce. The nurse count covers only nurses employed in public hospitals, so it understates it.28

Family doctors

GP density doubled between 2010 and 2023, from 0.19 to 0.38 per 1,000 people, as public primary care expanded.2

Greece produced 100.6 nursing graduates per 100,000 people in 2023, more than twice the EU average of 38.6.2 Keeping trained people in the country is the same problem the brain-drain pillar tracks for the wider economy.

Public health spending per person has grown faster than the EU's

Public health spending per person in Greece grew 26% in real terms between 2015 and 2023, slightly faster than the EU's 24%.9

Health spending was one of the casualties of the debt crisis and the bailout programmes. It fell from 9.6% of GDP in 2010 to 7.9% in 2014, while GDP itself was shrinking, and spending per person in purchasing power terms fell by about a quarter over the same years.9 The story of that period is told in the public debt pillar.

Since then spending per person has risen in almost every year, to 2,291 in purchasing power standards (a common unit that strips out price differences) in 2024.9 Public spending per person grew 26% in real terms between 2015 and 2023, slightly faster than the EU's 24%, and health has taken a steady 11.3–11.9% of all government spending since 2019.2

Screening, personal doctors, digital records and new hospitals

Greece is putting €1.5bn from the EU Recovery and Resilience Facility into health, 4.3% of its total allocation, alongside new screening programmes, a personal-doctor system, digital records and three donor-funded hospitals.2

The Recovery Fund money pays mainly for buildings and IT. The European Commission lists a €254m project for interventions in 80 public hospitals in all seven health regions, and a €199m project to upgrade 156 health centres.11, 12 In July 2026 Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis said more than 150 health centres had been fully upgraded and more than 80 hospitals had received major work, mostly to emergency departments, and in August the finance ministry put the number of completed renovations of hospitals and health centres at more than 230.13, 14 These are government figures, and the definitions differ from one statement to the next.

  • 2022The "personal doctor" system makes a GP or internist the first point of contact for every adult. It is widened in 2024 to private and rural doctors.2
  • Nov 2023Construction starts at Komotini, the first of three new public hospitals funded by the Stavros Niarchos Foundation (SNF) and designed by Renzo Piano, with Sparta and a children's hospital in Thessaloniki to follow.15, 16
  • 2024The "Prolamvano" ("I prevent") screening plan offers free breast, cervical and colorectal cancer screening and heart checks, funded by the Recovery Fund. A single digital waiting list for surgery in state hospitals also starts.2
  • May 2025A fully operational electronic health record goes live: patients can see diagnoses, prescriptions, test results and hospital records through a central portal or the MyHealth app, where e-prescriptions also arrive.2
  • Jun 2025Adults who had not chosen a personal doctor are enrolled automatically. In May the health ministry counted 5,115,264 people registered and 5,496 personal doctors.17
  • May 2026The government says Prolamvano has reached more than 6 million people, including about 1.21m digital mammograms, and commits €300m to extend it to 2030.18
  • End-2027SNF's target to complete and hand over all three hospitals. The three cost more than €500m.15

The new SNF hospitals are among the larger public projects in the country and are tracked with the others in the infrastructure pillar; they will also carry rooftop solar panels, part of the wider energy transition.15 The digital side, from e-prescriptions to the health record, draws on the same state digitalisation drive covered in the tech pillar, and 38% of Greeks accessed their health information online in 2024, against 28% in the EU.2

What to watch

Whether the reforms bring down what patients pay and reach the people who now go without care.

  • Out-of-pocket costs. Households paid 34.2% of Greek health spending directly in 2024, against 15.1% in the EU, and the state 61%, against about 80%. The share has not moved below 33% in a decade; Eurostat's 2025 health accounts are the next reading.92
  • Unmet need. In 2025, 11.5% of adults said they had gone without a medical examination they needed, mostly because of cost, against 2.4% in the EU. The 2026 EU-SILC survey, published by Eurostat in 2027, is the clearest test of whether the reforms reach them.10
  • Spending. Current health spending was 8.4% of GDP in 2024, against 10.2% for the EU.9
  • Nurse hiring. Greece has 3.8 public-hospital nurses per 1,000 people, against 8.5 in the EU, while it trains more than twice the EU rate; the test is whether the public system absorbs them.28
  • The SNF hospitals' handover at the end of 2027, and whether they open fully staffed: SNF says staffing depends on commitments made by the Greek state.15
  • Funding after the Recovery Fund: Greece aims to absorb its full €36bn allocation by the end of 2026, so the running costs of the new facilities fall to the national budget, for which the draft 2027 budget forecasts a primary surplus (a surplus before interest payments) of 3.3% of GDP.1419
