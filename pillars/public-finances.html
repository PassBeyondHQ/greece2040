Greek debt has fallen 64 points of GDP in five years, mostly because the economy grew

Greece's public debt fell from a peak of 209.4% of GDP in 2020 to 145.4% in 2025, and the budget swung from a deficit of 9.9% of GDP to a surplus of 2.0% (Eurostat, Oct 2026). Debt in euros barely changed: the ratio fell because nominal GDP grew by about half. Greece is still the most indebted country in the euro area, and the IMF projects 115.3% by 2030.

Debt down 64 points of GDP in five years

Greek general government debt fell from 209.4% of GDP in 2020, its peak, to 145.4% in 2025, a drop of 64.0 percentage points in five years.1

"General government" covers the central state, local authorities and the social security funds, and "gross debt" is what Eurostat counts for the EU's excessive deficit procedure: loans, bonds and bills at face value, without subtracting the state's cash. Dividing it by a year's GDP gives the debt ratio, the number markets and the rating agencies watch most closely.

The fall went year by year: 197.3% in 2021, 177.8% in 2022, 164.0% in 2023, 153.5% in 2024 and 145.4% in 2025. For context, the ratio was 179.6% in 2015, the year of the third bailout, and 104.6% in 2007, before the crisis.1

The latest quarterly figure is lower still. Eurostat put Greek debt at 140.8% of GDP at the end of June 2026, down from 152.0% a year earlier. That 11.2-point fall was the largest of any EU country over the year, ahead of Cyprus (9.6 points), on Eurostat's quarterly figures.3

Which vintage?

Greek debt ratios shift when GDP is revised. ELSTAT's benchmark revision of the national accounts in October 2026 raised the level of GDP, so older documents quote 146.1% (Eurostat, April 2026) or 146.6% (IMF, October 2026) for 2025. This page uses Eurostat's October 2026 vintage throughout, unless a forecast is labelled otherwise.14

Why did the ratio fall when the debt itself did not?

Measured in euros, Greek public debt rose slightly, from €350.8bn in 2020 to €363.0bn in 2025, so the ratio fell because nominal GDP grew from €167.5bn to €249.7bn, an increase of 49%.12

A debt ratio has two parts, and in Greece only the bottom one moved much. Debt peaked in euros at €369.1bn in 2023 and has edged down since, but the change between 2020 and 2025 was +€12.2bn, or 3.5%. Nominal GDP, which is real output multiplied by prices, rose much faster.

Splitting the 64 points

We broke the fall into its parts using the standard debt-dynamics identity, with Eurostat's figures for each year from 2021 to 2025. The ratio rises with interest and with primary deficits, and falls as nominal GDP grows. Anything else, such as using cash to repay loans, ends up in "other".

  • Real growth took off 36.4 points. Real GDP grew a cumulative 21.9% over the five years, with 8.7% in 2021 and 5.5% in 2022 as the economy bounced back from the pandemic. See economic growth.
  • Inflation took off 34.6 points. The GDP deflator, the price level of everything the economy produces, rose 22.2%, with 6.3% in 2022 and 6.5% in 2023. Most Greek debt pays fixed rates set years ago, so higher prices shrank its real weight.
  • Interest added 14.9 points, at between 2.5% and 3.5% of GDP a year.
  • Primary balances took off a net 6.9 points: deficits in 2021 and 2022 added debt, and surpluses from 2023 more than offset them.
  • Other items took off about 1.0 point.
In plain terms

If Greece had kept its 2020 debt of €350.8bn unchanged, the 2025 ratio would have been 140.5%. The actual figure, 145.4%, is close to that: a household whose income rose by half while its mortgage stayed the same would see the same thing.

What this number doesn't say

Inflation is a one-off help. It cut the ratio because debt carried old fixed rates while prices jumped in 2022–2023, and that does not repeat once inflation settles near 2%. From here, the fall has to come more from primary surpluses and real growth.

From deficit to surplus

Greece ran a general government surplus of 2.0% of GDP in 2025, about €4.9bn, after a deficit of 9.9% in 2020, when the state paid for pandemic support.1

The "headline" balance is what is left after all spending, including interest, is set against all revenue. Greece reached a small surplus in 2016–2019, fell back into deficit in the pandemic and returned to surplus in 2024 (1.4%). In 2025 the euro area as a whole ran a deficit of 3.0% of GDP and Italy one of 3.1%.5

The primary balance: the number the creditors watched

The primary balance is the budget balance before interest payments. It shows whether the state lives within its means on current policy, and it was the target written into the bailout programmes. On Eurostat's figures, Greece's primary surplus was 4.9% of GDP in 2024 and 5.1% in 2025.1

How the budget got there

Most of the swing came from spending falling back as the pandemic support ended, while revenue held up. General government spending was 59.3% of GDP in 2020 and 47.8% in 2025, and revenue rose from 49.4% to 49.8%.20

Definitions differ

The bailout programmes and the Greek budget use their own definition of the primary balance, which leaves out some one-off items, so official figures can differ from ours by a few tenths of a point.

How did Greek debt get so high?

Greece already had debt of about 105% of GDP before the crisis, and between 2008 and 2013 it then ran double-digit deficits while its economy shrank by more than a quarter, so the ratio jumped from 110.9% in 2008 to 175.1% in 2011.12

The euro brought low interest rates, but Greek deficits stayed large. On Eurostat's revised figures, the budget deficit never fell below 4% of GDP between 2000 and 2008 and was 6.8% in 2007, when the euro-area average was below 1%.1

In October 2009 the newly elected government told Eurostat that the 2009 deficit would be 12.5% of GDP, not the 3.7% previously reported, and raised the 2008 figure from 5.0% to 7.7%. The Commission later found severe problems with Greek fiscal statistics. Trust collapsed, borrowing costs rose, and by spring 2010 Greece could no longer finance itself in the markets.12

  • Deficits. The deficit reached 15.4% of GDP in 2009, and debt in euros rose from €239.9bn in 2007 to €356.2bn in 2011.1
  • A shrinking economy. Nominal GDP fell from €238.7bn in 2008 to €174.4bn in 2016, a fall of 27%, as output collapsed and prices fell. Unemployment rose to 27.8% in 2013; see jobs and unemployment.2
  • Rising interest costs. The interest bill reached 7.7% of GDP in 2011, and Greek 10-year bond yields rose so far that the market closed to Greece; see bond yields and the spread.1
The denominator problem

The mechanism now cutting the ratio worked in reverse after 2009. Austerity cut the deficit but also cut GDP, so a debt of about €315–320bn stayed near 180% of a €175bn economy in 2015 and 2016.

Three bailouts and the largest debt restructuring on record

Between 2010 and 2018 Greece borrowed from the euro area and the IMF under three rescue programmes, and in 2012 private bondholders accepted a 53.5% cut in the face value of their bonds, which removed about €107bn of debt.1315

The loans replaced market borrowing that Greece could no longer get, and came with conditions: spending cuts, tax rises and reforms. That is why most Greek debt today is owed to other governments rather than to investors.

  • Oct 2009The new government's notification to Eurostat revised the planned 2009 deficit from 3.7% to 12.5% of GDP, and 2008 from 5.0% to 7.7%. The Commission later found "severe irregularities" in Greek fiscal statistics.12
  • May 2010First programme, €110bn: €80bn of bilateral loans from euro-area countries (the Greek Loan Facility, or GLF) and a €30bn IMF Stand-By Arrangement.1314
  • Mar 2012Second programme, €164.5bn: €144.7bn from the euro area's rescue fund (the EFSF) and €19.8bn from the IMF. At the same time, private bondholders swapped €199.2bn of Greek bonds, with 96.9% participation, in the "PSI" (private sector involvement).1315
  • Dec 2012A debt buyback used €11.3bn of EFSF money to retire €31.9bn of bonds, a further net cut of €20.6bn.15
  • Aug 2015Third programme, from the European Stability Mechanism (ESM): up to €86bn, of which €61.9bn was drawn.1316
  • Jun 2018The Eurogroup agreed debt relief: a 10-year extension of maturities and a 10-year deferral of interest and repayments on part of the EFSF loans.13
  • Aug 2018Greece left the last programme on 20 August. Enhanced surveillance by the Commission followed and ended on 20 Aug 2022; lighter post-programme checks continue.13
−€51bn

Fall in Greek debt in euros in 2012, from €356.2bn to €305.1bn, the only big drop in the series. Without the haircut and buyback, the ratio would have climbed far above its 2020 peak.1

The PSI cut the debt but did not end the crisis. It hit Greek banks and pension funds, which held Greek bonds, and the banks then needed public money; that is part of the 13.6% deficit in 2013. The bank clean-up is covered in bad loans and the banks. By 2015 the ratio was back at 179.6%, because the economy kept shrinking.1

Greek debt is large, but long, fixed and cheap

About three-quarters of Greek public debt is owed to official creditors on long, low, fixed-rate terms, so Greece pays less interest relative to GDP than Italy despite owing more.101

That is why a ratio of 145% is easier to carry than it looks: little of it falls due soon, none of it moves with market rates, and the state holds a large cash reserve.

Interest: high debt, moderate bill

Interest payments came to 3.1% of GDP in 2025, against 3.8% in Italy and 1.9% in the euro area, and well below the 7.7% Greece paid in 2011.1 On a cash basis the bill was lower still, 2.1% of GDP, because part of the interest on EFSF loans is deferred under the 2018 deal and added to the debt instead of being paid.7

Paying back the rescue loans early

Since 2022 Greece has used its cash to repay official loans ahead of schedule. Each early repayment lowers gross debt in euros and future interest, and the IMF reports a plan to clear all the first-bailout GLF loans by 2031, ten years before their final maturity in 2041.11

  • Apr 2022The last IMF loans, about €1.86bn, repaid about two years early. Greece no longer owes the IMF anything.17
  • Dec 2022 – Dec 2025Early GLF repayments of €2.6bn (2022), €5.3bn (2023), €7.9bn (2024) and €5.3bn (2025).7
  • Jun 2026A fifth early GLF repayment of €6.9bn.7
  • Oct 2026€2.5bn of EFSF loans repaid early, funded from the sale of state stakes in banks. Early repayments in 2026 total €12.6bn on the budget's figures.7

The GLF balance was €19.4bn at the end of October 2026.7 The cash for these payments came mainly from the buffer left over from the 2018 ESM programme, which the budget report says has now been fully used.7

What this number doesn't say

Cheap official loans are a legacy, not a permanent feature. As they are repaid and replaced with bonds at market rates, the average cost rises: new market borrowing in 2026 (to September) cost 2.92% on average, against 1.44% on the existing stock.9 Investors who lend at those rates are watching Greece's credit ratings and the spread over German bonds.

Greece is still the most indebted country in the euro area

At 145.4% of GDP in 2025, Greek debt was the highest in the euro area, ahead of Italy at 136.7% and France at 115.7%, and well above the euro-area figure of 87.7% for the 20 countries that used the euro in 2025.5

The gap is closing from both sides. Greece's ratio has fallen 64.0 points since 2020, while Italy's has come down only from 154.7% to 136.7% and has been rising again since 2023. France's has risen to 115.7% from 98.2% in 2019.1

The race with Italy

On quarterly data, the two are now almost level. At the end of June 2026 Eurostat recorded 140.8% for Greece and 139.9% for Italy, a gap of 0.9 points, against 14.4 points a year earlier.3 On the IMF's October 2026 forecasts, Italy's ratio (137.9%) would be above Greece's (136.0%) for 2026 as a whole, which would end a run at the top that Greece has held every year since 2007 on Eurostat's current figures.4 That is a forecast, and the final figure will come in Eurostat's April 2027 notification.

Where is the debt ratio heading?

All three main forecasters expect the ratio to keep falling, and the IMF, the only one with a path beyond 2028, projects 115.3% of GDP by 2030 and 111.1% by 2031.4

These forecasts use different data vintages and assumptions, so they differ by up to 2.2 points for 2027.

Forecasts are not results

These paths assume steady growth of about 1.5–2.3% a year, continued primary surpluses and no new shock. In 2020 a single bad year added 26 points to the ratio.1

What to watch

The easy part of the fall is over: inflation has eased, the EU recovery money ends in 2026, and the surplus is forecast to shrink, so the debt ratio will now fall more slowly and depend more on growth and policy.67

1. A smaller surplus

The government's 2027 budget plans a primary surplus of 3.6% of GDP in 2026 and 3.2% in 2027, down from 5.1% in 2025, with a headline surplus of 0.6% and then 0.1%.7 The Commission forecasts headline surpluses of 0.9% in 2026, 0.7% in 2027 and 0.2% in 2028.6 The IMF is more cautious and projects a small deficit, 0.7% of GDP, from 2027.4 Part of the room is going on tax cuts and new benefits voted in November 2026.

2. Interest costs creeping up

The IMF expects Greece's effective interest rate to rise from 2.0% in 2025 to 2.8% by 2034, as cheap official loans give way to bonds sold at market rates.11 The Commission sees interest spending rising by 0.2% of GDP in 2028.6 Deferred EFSF interest is also being added to the debt: €17.5bn by the end of 2026 and €19.6bn by the end of 2027, on the budget's estimates.7

3. The EU fiscal rules

Under the EU's reformed rules, each country agrees a ceiling on the growth of its "net expenditure" (spending excluding interest, EU-funded programmes and one-offs, net of new tax measures). The Council endorsed Greece's plan for 2025–2028 on 21 Jan 2025, and on 8 Jul 2025 allowed up to 1.5% of GDP of extra defence spending under a national escape clause, later extended to energy-resilience investment in October 2026.187 The budget projects net expenditure growth of 8.9% in 2026 against a plan figure of 3.6%, and says it stays within the cumulative ceiling thanks to the escape clause.7

4. Life after the Recovery and Resilience Facility

Greece's recovery plan, "Greece 2.0", is worth €18.22bn in EU grants and €17.73bn in cheap EU loans, and it ends in 2026.19 By the time the 2027 budget was submitted, €24.6bn of it had arrived.7 The Commission expects Greek growth to slow to 1.5% in 2027 and 2028 partly because these funds run out.6 Slower growth means a slower fall in the debt ratio; see economic growth and foreign direct investment for what could replace the EU money.

5. Losing the top spot

The Finance Ministry expects 2026 to be the first year in two decades in which Greece does not have the highest debt ratio in the EU, and Eurostat's quarterly data shows Italy almost level.73 That is a forecast until Eurostat publishes the 2026 figures in April 2027.

The takeaway

Greece has cut its debt ratio by more percentage points than any other euro-area country since 2020, mostly through growth and inflation, while keeping a primary surplus and a long, cheap, fixed-rate debt profile. The ratio is still the highest in the euro area, and on the IMF's path the next 30 points take about five years and depend on growth and policy, with little help from inflation.
