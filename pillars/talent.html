More Greeks now arrive than leave, but the crisis loss is far from repaired

In 2024, 51,993 Greek citizens settled in Greece and 32,141 left, a net gain of 19,852 and the second positive year in a row, according to ELSTAT estimates published by Eurostat. Departures have more than halved since the 2012 peak of 70,696. The gain is still small: 2023–24 added about 29,000 Greek citizens, against a net loss of about 328,000 in 2010–2022.

More Greeks now arrive than leave

In 2024, 51,993 Greek citizens moved to Greece to live for a year or more and 32,141 moved abroad, so arrivals outnumbered departures for the second year running.12

The figures are ELSTAT's annual estimates, published by Eurostat in its emigration and immigration datasets and filtered to people holding Greek citizenship. Departures peaked at 70,696 in 2012, at the height of the debt crisis, and have fallen in almost every year since 2016, to the lowest level since 2009.

Arrivals moved the other way. They held at about 26,000–34,000 a year through the 2010s, dropped to 20,864 in the pandemic year of 2020, and then rose to 46,091 in 2023 and 51,993 in 2024, roughly double the level of 2012–13.

What "arriving" counts

"Arriving" means every Greek citizen who settles in Greece for at least 12 months. That includes emigrants coming home, but also children and grandchildren of the diaspora born abroad, and people who have left and returned more than once. It does not follow the same people over time, so it cannot say what share of the crisis emigrants came back.

Counted by country of birth instead of citizenship, 44,336 people born in Greece arrived in 2024.3 The OECD notes that inflows by citizenship have grown faster than inflows by birth, which suggests a growing share of Greek citizens born abroad.5 In 2024, 12.7% of arriving Greek citizens (6,609) were under 15.2

The balance turned positive in 2023, but the hole is still deep

+19,852 more Greek citizens arrived than left in 2024, the second positive year in a row and the first since 2009.12

The balance was negative every year from 2010 to 2022, by more than 40,000 a year in 2012 and 2013. It narrowed steadily after 2016, reached −8,134 in 2022, and flipped to +9,160 in 2023. The OECD's July 2026 review of Greek emigrants describes 2023 as the first year since the crisis in which inflows of Greek citizens exceeded outflows.4

−328,266

Net loss of Greek citizens, 2010–2022 (703,224 left, 374,958 arrived). The two positive years since then added 29,012.

Over the full 2010–2024 period, 772,296 Greek citizens left and 473,042 arrived. Those are the sums behind the widely reported "775,000 left and about 470,000 came back" over 15 years. Since the arrivals include people who never lived in Greece before, the ratio between the two is not a return rate.

What this number doesn't say

At the 2024 pace, the net loss of 2010–2022 would take another 15 years or so to offset, and a single year can move a lot: ELSTAT's figures are survey-based estimates, not a population register, and they are revised. The 2020 figures are distorted by the pandemic. About 32,000 Greek citizens still left in 2024, so the outflow has slowed rather than stopped.

Who left, and where they went

The crisis emigrants were mostly young and far better educated than earlier waves, and they went mainly to Germany, the UK and, increasingly, the Netherlands.

The Bank of Greece estimated in 2016 that 427,000 residents of Greece left in 2008–2013, of whom about 223,000 were aged 25–39.6 Its author, Sophia Lazaretou, described them as educated people with at least two years of work experience, heading mainly for Germany, the UK and the United Arab Emirates. She also cited an Endeavor Greece survey (2014) in which 46% of respondents aged 18–34 were considering a move abroad.

A brain drain, in the literal sense

  • Among Greek-born emigrants who had lived in an OECD country for under five years in 2020/21, 49% were highly educated (a tertiary degree); among those abroad for more than a decade, 25% were.7
  • Greek-born doctors working in other OECD countries rose from about 2,900 in 2000/01 to almost 10,000 in 2020/21, equal to 13% of all Greek doctors at home and abroad, one of the highest shares in the OECD.7 The domestic side of that story is on our health page.
  • The age profile has barely changed: people aged 25–39 made up 46.0% of everyone who emigrated from Greece in 2013 and 44.8% in 2024 (all citizenships).8

More than 800,000 Greek-born people live in OECD countries, and Germany alone hosts about 237,000 of them, many from the guest-worker generation of the 1960s.4 That older community is less educated: only about one in five Greek-born residents of Germany holds a degree, against 68% of those in the UK and 72% in France.7

The flows have since shifted. Germany recorded 18,867 Greek citizens arriving in 2014 and 8,833 in 2024. The number of Greek citizens living in the Netherlands rose from 8,584 in 2011 to 32,924 in 2025, a near fourfold rise that the OECD links partly to students choosing Dutch universities after Brexit.9104

Who is coming back, and why

Returnees are younger and much better educated than Greeks who never left, and most come back from elsewhere in Europe.

The OECD analysed returnees counted in ELSTAT's 2021 census, which asks people whether they lived abroad and why they came back.5

  • Young: 54% of those who returned in 2016–2021 were aged 20–39.
  • Educated: 60% hold a tertiary degree, against 23% of their peers who never emigrated, and 47% work as professionals, against 18% of non-migrants.
  • From Europe: 83% had lived elsewhere in Europe, and Germany and the UK together account for 43% of returnees.
  • Urban: 42% settle in Attica and 18% in Central Macedonia (Thessaloniki), and four in five return to their home region.
  • Work comes second: "repatriation" (coming home as such) is the main reason given by over half; 18–22% return for a job, depending on the period.
Reintegration takes time

In the 2021 census, 72% of returnees who had been back five years were in work, against 46% of those who had just returned. Unemployment was 12% among returnees with a doctorate and 15% among those with a master's degree.5

The pay gap is smaller than it looks, but it is still there

A single worker on the average wage took home €21,498 after tax in Greece in 2025, against €36,535 in Germany, or 59%.11 Prices are lower in Greece (83% of the EU average in 2024, against 109% in Germany), so in purchasing power standards (a unit that removes price differences) Greek take-home pay is 74% of Germany's and 85% of the EU average.12 Wage trends are covered on our jobs page.

What surveys of Greeks at home and abroad say

Two recent surveys, both summarised by the OECD, fill in motives. Neither is a random sample, so they show attitudes rather than totals.5

  • Returnees (Kapa Research for the National Documentation Centre, EKT, Oct 2024, 602 people who worked abroad in 2010–2019): 50% work in the private sector and 26% are self-employed or business owners, and 7% work remotely for employers abroad. ICT (10%) and education (10%) are among the main sectors. 29% earn €1,001–1,500 a month, and 46% report difficulty adjusting to life back in Greece.
  • Greeks aged 20–50 at home and abroad (Metron Analysis, 2025, 848 people): 85% of those abroad said they were unlikely to return within two years, rising to 92% among 20–30-year-olds. Better pay (52%), tax relief (36%) and work–life balance (25%) were the most cited reasons that could change their mind, while limited trust in institutions and doubts about meritocracy were the main deterrents.

Technology is one of the draws: ICT is among the main sectors in the EKT sample, and returnees are over-represented in ICT, science and engineering in the census. The growth of Greek startup funding and the foreign investment behind new jobs are covered on their own pages.

What the state offers returnees

The main incentive is a 50% cut in income tax for seven years for people who move their tax residence to Greece, alongside a jobs platform and faster recognition of doctors' qualifications.

  • 2019The Labour Ministry launches Rebrain Greece, a platform that matches skilled Greeks abroad with jobs at Greek companies, promoted through events in London, Amsterdam, New York and Munich.13
  • 2020Law 4758/2020 halves income tax on Greek employment or business income for seven years, for people who were not Greek tax residents in five of the previous six years, move from the EU/EEA or a country with a tax co-operation agreement, work for a Greek employer or business in Greece and commit to stay at least two years.13
  • April 2025Law 5194/2025 recognises US board-certified specialties of Greek doctors automatically; an October 2025 amendment extends this to Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Switzerland and the UK.13

Does it work? The evidence is thin

The government has published no figures on how many people use the tax break, and it has not been formally evaluated, the OECD notes. In the EKT survey, 14% of returnees said they had benefited from tax incentives, which the OECD reads as a sign that money alone is not the main driver of return.135

Rebrain Greece's own counter showed about 2,600 applications from skilled workers, 1,200 job postings and 136 confirmed hires at the end of February 2026. Those are platform totals rather than confirmed moves from abroad.1314

Timing is not proof

Arrivals rose after these measures came in, but they also rose as unemployment fell and wages climbed, and after the pandemic made remote work common. No study has yet separated the effect of the incentives from the rest.

The push that drove people out has eased

Youth unemployment fell from 59.2% in 2013 to 19.1% in 2025, easing the pressure behind the crisis-era exodus, though it is still above the EU's 15.2%.15

At the peak, more than half of Greeks aged 15–24 who wanted a job could not find one. The rate has fallen every year since 2013 except during the pandemic, and in 2025 the gap to the EU was 3.9 points, against 34 points in 2013. Our jobs page covers the youth labour market and overall unemployment in detail.

The share of 15–29-year-olds neither in work nor in education or training (the NEET rate) halved from 28.5% in 2013 to 13.6% in 2025, still 2.6 points above the EU's 11.0%.16

Degrees: ahead of the EU for a decade, behind in 2025

42.8% of Greeks aged 25–34 held a tertiary degree in 2025, below the EU's 44.8% for the first time since 2011.17

The share climbed from 30.6% in 2010 to a peak of 45.2% in 2022, and was above the EU average every year from 2012 to 2024. The 2025 drop of 1.7 points is a single year in a survey-based series and may partly reflect sampling, but it is the latest figure and it puts Greece behind.

Keeping students, and attracting new ones

Just under one in 20 Greek tertiary students was enrolled abroad in 2023, above the OECD average, and the UK remains the top destination, although enrolments there fell sharply in 2022 while the Netherlands and Bulgaria gained.4 Greek universities are now adding degrees taught in other languages: in Thessaloniki, Aristotle University has 8 accredited and 7 pending, and the International Hellenic University runs 6, according to a November 2026 report.18

Why every returnee counts: a shrinking working-age population

Greece had 6.02 million people aged 20–64 in 2025, about 771,000 fewer than in 2010, and Eurostat projects a further fall of about one million by 2040.1920

Emigration is only part of the cause. Greece recorded 65,439 births and 120,825 deaths in 2025, and births have fallen by almost half since 2008 (118,302).21 The fertility rate was 1.24 children per woman in 2024.22

In plain terms

A net gain of 20,000 Greek citizens a year, most of them of working age, offsets only a fraction of a working-age population that the projection has shrinking by roughly 68,000 a year to 2040. Returns help, but they cannot reverse ageing on their own, which matters for growth and for the pension bill.

About the projection

The projection is Eurostat's 2023 baseline and starts from a slightly lower 2025 level (5.98 million) than the recorded figure, so compare the two lines by direction rather than by exact gap. It is a forecast, and it depends on assumptions about births and migration.

What to watch

Whether the positive balance holds as Europe's labour markets cool and Greek wages keep catching up, and whether the data improve enough to tell returnees from new arrivals.

  • The 2025 migration estimates, which Eurostat normally publishes in late 2026 or early 2027: a third positive year would show 2023–24 was not a one-off.
  • Departures, still about 32,000 Greek citizens a year. A continued fall matters as much as rising arrivals.
  • Pay: Greek take-home pay is 85% of the EU average after adjusting for prices. Narrowing that gap is the reason most often cited by emigrants who might return.
  • Tertiary attainment: whether 2025's dip below the EU average reverses in the 2026 data.
  • Uptake of the 50% tax break: the OECD recommends publishing results and evaluating the incentives, which would show whether they move people.
  • Doctors: how many use the new fast-track recognition of foreign specialist titles, given the shortage of staff in public hospitals.
  • Work rules: the Labour Ministry's November 2026 bill on AI and algorithmic management at work, which affects the remote and platform jobs some returnees hold.23
